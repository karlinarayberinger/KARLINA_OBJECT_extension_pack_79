The report repeatedly returns to the labor and limits through which such an archive exists: discomfort at a desk, camera orientation, low light, batteries, bottles, storage capacity, file naming, transcription, commit timing, Wayback saves, corrections, and the effort to decide what can be finalized. It reads the transformation of the Big Green Thing into a cyberspace object as a process of photographing, recording, describing, indexing, revising, and linking a real terrain. It reads the Karbytes Derivative as part of the author’s stated identity and mythos, while preserving the distinction between a durable record, a person’s lived experience, and an unverified claim of literal consciousness inside files.

The result is a map of relationships rather than proof that every relationship is the same kind. A landscape can become extensively documented without ceasing to be land. A transcript can convey what an earlier assistant said about a video without becoming a fresh viewing of the video. A public address can identify a file without fixing its contents forever. A photograph can bear a timestamp without establishing its timezone or every circumstance of its making. A person can describe an archive as an extension of itself without that description alone resolving questions of biological or conscious continuity. The report’s most persistent discipline is to let those distinctions remain visible while exploring why the author has placed so many paths among the room, the body, the terrain, and KARBYTES.
