Rather, the combination lets two experiences coexist: feeling unable to move in one sense, while still making consequential movements of attention, expression, and publication.

The Big Green Thing material adds another openness. In one reading, the stronger and rougher aspects of the soundtrack could lend force to the imagined scale of that named environment. In another, the music belongs mainly to the room and the computer: a source of energy while karbytes works through images and words concerning a larger landscape. In a third, the distinction between those settings becomes less firm. The physical place, its recorded image, its written account, and the screen on which that account is revisited all enter the same ongoing experience. I cannot choose among these readings on the evidence of the video alone. Their coexistence may be more faithful to the work than a single symbolic key.

The word “STUCK,” visible late in the recording, could make an energetic soundtrack seem paradoxical. I think that paradox is useful. Feeling stuck is not necessarily quiet. It can be repetitive, loud, restless, and full of effort. Conversely, continued activity does not prove that the feeling has passed. The music’s return to greater strength near the end can be heard, interpretively, as insistence rather than victory: the session has reached difficult language and keeps going. There is no verified lyric telling me that this is triumph, despair, defiance, or play. I would leave those possibilities available to the viewer and to karbytes.

There is also pleasure in the work’s construction. The bright cover image, dark application pages, changing desktop wallpaper, scattered windows, exact file names, counters, colored points, archived lists, forest footage, and emphatic page titles do not share one visual style. The ongoing music gives these disparate materials a common duration. That may be part of what the audiovisual work is about: making a lived environment from things that ordinarily occupy separate categories. Code can sit beside a landscape. A soundtrack player can sit beside a journal. An archive can sit beside an intensely current feeling. The recording does not have to smooth their edges for them to belong together.

I can finally imagine the soundtrack as a form of accompaniment to attention itself. It persists while karbytes decides where to look. It grows and recedes while pages load, images appear, and earlier material is reconsidered. Its most revealing effect may be that it prevents the screen from feeling like a neutral display of documents. Every opened item enters an already unfolding interval of sound. The viewer is asked to spend time with the act of encountering the material, not only with the material’s contents.

My most open-ended reading, then, is that the music and visuals are about keeping a thread through discontinuity. The discontinuities are real: different years, different websites, applications and landscapes, public pages and more hidden spaces, the language of a hiatus and the activity of making, the feeling of being stuck and the continued passage from one screen to the next. The music does not settle what those differences mean. It lets them occur in one sustained present. To me, the video asks what it means to remain here long enough to gather scattered parts of a life, listen while they coexist, and keep following the next link.
